## How to use Level 4

Take these mocks only after finishing Levels 1 to 3 on all 10 topics. Each mock mixes every topic, the way the real paper does.

- **On the website:** press Start on a mock. It works like the exam's CBT screen: 25 questions, 25 minutes, one question at a time, a question palette and Mark for review. When the time runs out, the test submits itself.
- **Marking:** +1 for a right answer, −1/3 for a wrong one, 0 for a skip. That is the penalty in RRB JE, Kerala PSC and SSC JE Paper 2.
- **When to guess:** a blind guess among four options gains nothing on average (1/4 × 1 − 3/4 × 1/3 = 0). Rule out one option and a guess among the other three is worth +1/9 of a mark. Rule out two and it is worth +1/3. So guess only after you have eliminated at least one option.
- **After the test:** the review shows every question with your answer, the right one and the full solution. Read why each wrong option is wrong, not just the right one. Weak topics link back to the same section in Levels 1 to 3.
- **Reading here instead:** the solution sits right under each question, so cover it before you answer.
- **Retakes:** wait about a week. An immediate retake mostly tests your memory of the answers.

Almost every Material Science question tests one fact: a crystal structure and its numbers, a point on the iron-carbon diagram, what a heat treatment produces, what an alloy contains or which test finds which flaw. ## Speed toolkit

Most Material Science questions in RRB JE, SSC JE and Kerala PSC need one fact. Learn the values below by heart, and a two-minute question takes 30 seconds.

| HCP | 6 atoms per hexagonal cell, CN 12, APF 0.74, ideal c/a = 1.633 |
| Common metals | BCC: α-iron, chromium, tungsten, molybdenum, vanadium. FCC: γ-iron, aluminium, copper, nickel, silver, gold, lead. HCP: magnesium, zinc, titanium, cadmium, cobalt, beryllium. |
| Crystal systems | Cubic: a = b = c, all angles 90°. Tetragonal: a = b ≠ c, all 90°. Orthorhombic: a ≠ b ≠ c, all 90°. Hexagonal: a = b ≠ c, 90°, 90° and 120°. Monoclinic: one angle not 90°. Triclinic: no sides or angles equal. |
| Defects by dimension | Point (0): vacancy, interstitial, substitutional atom, Schottky, Frenkel. Line (1): edge and screw dislocations. Surface (2): grain boundaries, twin boundaries, stacking faults. Volume (3): voids, cracks, inclusions. |
| Iron-carbon points | Eutectoid 727 °C, 0.8% C: austenite → pearlite. Eutectic 1147 °C, 4.3% C: liquid → ledeburite. Peritectic 1493 °C, about 0.17% C. Older papers write 723 °C, 0.77%, 1130 °C or 4.33%. |
| Iron-carbon limits | Ferrite holds at most 0.022% C, austenite 2.11% (2% in most papers), and cementite is 6.67% C. Steel goes up to 2% C, cast iron from 2 to 6.67%. |
| Changes in pure iron | α (BCC) turns to γ (FCC) at 910 °C, γ to δ (BCC) at 1400 °C, and iron melts at 1539 °C. It stops being magnetic at 768 °C. |
| Phases and their cells | Ferrite BCC, austenite FCC, cementite orthorhombic, martensite BCT. Pearlite is ferrite + cementite in layers. Ledeburite is austenite + cementite, formed at the eutectic. |
| Basic heat treatments | Annealing: furnace cool, softest, coarse pearlite. Normalising: air cool, fine pearlite. Hardening: quench to martensite, about RC 65. Tempering: reheat below 727 °C, so toughness rises and brittleness and internal stress fall. |
| Isothermal treatments | Austempering, or isothermal quenching, gives bainite. Martempering, or stepped quenching, gives martensite with less distortion and cracking. |
| Case hardening | Carburising adds carbon at about 900–950 °C. Nitriding adds nitrogen from ammonia at about 500–550 °C, with no quench. Cyaniding, or liquid carbonitriding, adds carbon and nitrogen from a cyanide bath at about 800–870 °C. Flame and induction hardening change only the surface structure, not its chemistry. |
| Steels | Tool steel 0.5–1.5% C. Rails: high carbon steel. 18/8 stainless: 18% chromium, 8% nickel; chromium makes it stainless. HSS 18-4-1: 18% tungsten, 4% chromium, 1% vanadium. |
| Cast irons | Grey: graphite flakes, best damping, strong in compression. White: carbon as cementite. Malleable: anneal white iron. Nodular or SG: add magnesium or cerium. | Composites | Matrix: a polymer (epoxy, polyester), a metal (aluminium, magnesium, titanium) or a ceramic. Reinforcement: glass, carbon or Kevlar fibres, or particles. |

| Fatigue and impact | The S-N curve of steel flattens at about 10⁶ to 10⁷ cycles, and that stress is the endurance limit. Izod and Charpy break a notched bar to measure toughness. |

### Relations that save a step

| Relation | Use it when |
| --- | --- |
| BCC: a = 4r/√3. FCC: a = 2√2 r, so r = a√2/4. | Atomic radius from the lattice parameter, or the other way round |
| APF = n × (4/3)πr³ / a³ | Packing factor, with n atoms per cell. It gives 0.52, 0.68 and 0.74. |
| F = C − P + 2, and F = C − P + 1 at fixed pressure | Gibbs phase rule. At a binary eutectic C = 2 and P = 3, so F = 0. |
| Fraction of a phase = opposite arm ÷ whole tie line | Lever rule. Pearlite in a slowly cooled steel below 0.8% C: (C − 0.022)/(0.8 − 0.022), close to C/0.8. |
| Pearlite above 0.8% C = (6.67 − C)/(6.67 − 0.8) | Hypereutectoid steel. The rest is proeutectoid cementite at the grain boundaries. |
| J = −D dc/dx | Fick's first law, for steady diffusion. D is in m²/s. |
| D = D₀ exp(−Q/RT) | Diffusion rises steeply with temperature. Use R for Q per mole and k for Q per atom, with T in kelvin. |
| T(recrystallisation) ≈ 0.4 × T(melting), in kelvin | Above this temperature, working is hot working. Lead and tin recrystallise at room temperature. |
| BHN = 2P / \[πD(D − √(D² − d²))\] | Brinell hardness, with P in kgf and D and d in mm |
| Atomic sizes within 15% | Hume-Rothery's size rule. Wide substitutional solubility also needs the same crystal structure, similar electronegativity and the same valency. |

### Unit traps

- **Celsius or kelvin.** The 0.4 × T(melting) rule and the diffusion formula need kelvin. Iron melts at 1539 °C, or 1812 K, so it recrystallises near 450 °C.
- **Carbon by weight.** Carbon percentages on the iron-carbon diagram are by weight, not by number of atoms.
- **Hardness numbers.** Brinell and Vickers numbers are in kgf/mm², though usually written without a unit. A Rockwell number means nothing without its scale letter, as in RC 65.
- **Diffusivity.** D has the units of area per time, m²/s. The flux J is per square metre per second.
- **Radius or lattice parameter.** Check whether the options are written in r or in a before you pick.
- **Textbook numbers.** 723 or 727 °C, 0.77 or 0.8% C, 1130 or 1147 °C, 4.3 or 4.33%: options rarely contain both, so pick the one that is there.

### Eliminating options

- **Check the structure family.** Room-temperature iron, chromium and tungsten are BCC. Aluminium, copper and nickel are FCC. Magnesium, zinc and titanium are HCP. An option list that mixes families can usually be cut to one.
- **Hot or cold.** Cold working raises strength and hardness, lowers ductility, and gives a good finish and close tolerances. Hot working refines the grain, needs less force and leaves scale. A property on the wrong side rules an option out.
- **Surface or whole part.** Carburising, nitriding, cyaniding, carbonitriding, flame and induction hardening treat the surface. Annealing, normalising, hardening and tempering act on the whole part.
- **"Also known as" pairs.** Austempering is isothermal quenching. Martempering is stepped or interrupted quenching. Liquid carbonitriding is cyaniding. Nodular, spheroidal graphite and ductile cast iron are one material.
- **Statement and "NOT" questions.** Mark each statement true or false before you look at the options.

## Mock 1

25 questions, 25 minutes, +1 for a right answer and −1/3 for a wrong one. 2 questions come from Kerala PSC papers (2026), none from RRB JE and 23 from other exams.

**Q1. MP Sub Engineer (Mechanical), 9 July 2017 (Shift 2) · Topic 3.** Working of metal above the recrystallisation temperature is known as (a) Pressing (b) Forging (c) Cold working (d) Hot working

*Answer: (d) Hot working.*

Hot and cold working are told apart by the recrystallisation temperature, not by how hot the metal feels. Above it, new strain-free grains form as fast as the metal is deformed, so it does not work harden and needs less force. Below it, the grains stay distorted and the metal gets harder and stronger with every pass.

- **(a) Pressing and (b) Forging:** names of processes. Either can be done hot or cold.
- **(c) Cold working:** working below the recrystallisation temperature.

**The trap:** forging is usually done hot, so (b) looks right. The question asks for the class of working that the temperature defines, not for a process.

**Q2. UPPSC AE Mains Paper I (Mechanical) 2024, 29 September 2025 · Topic 8.** Consider the following statements: Cast Iron is difficult to weld because of: I. Low ductility II. Poor Fusion III. Tendency to crack on cooling. Which of the above statements are correct? (a) Only I and III (b) Only I and II (c) Only II and III (d) I, II and III

*Answer: (a) Only I and III.*

Cast iron has almost no ductility, so it cannot stretch to take up the shrinkage stresses as the weld cools. The fast cooling next to the weld also turns that zone into hard white iron and martensite, and the joint cracks. Statement II is false: cast iron melts at a lower temperature than steel (about 1150–1200 °C) and is very fluid, so fusing it is easy.

- **(b) and (c):** both include II, which is false.
- **(d):** includes II as well.

**The trap:** "poor fusion" sounds like a real welding defect. With cast iron the problem is never melting it; it is keeping it from cracking, which is why it is preheated and cooled slowly.

**Q3. UPRVUNL TG2 (Mechanical Fitter), 9 May 2015 · Topic 5.** Steel which contains 0.8% carbon is called (a) Hypoeutectoid steel (b) Hypereutectoid Steel (c) Eutectoid Steel (d) Cast iron

*Answer: (c) Eutectoid steel.*

0.8% C is the eutectoid composition. Cooled slowly, the whole of its austenite turns into pearlite at 727 °C, with no ferrite or cementite before it.

- **(a) Hypoeutectoid steel:** below 0.8% C. It cools to proeutectoid ferrite plus pearlite.
- **(b) Hypereutectoid steel:** from 0.8% to 2% C. It cools to proeutectoid cementite plus pearlite.
- **(d) Cast iron:** above 2% C.

**Memory aid:** hypo means under and hyper means over, as in hypothermia and hyperactive.

**Q4. UKPSC JE Mechanical 2013 (Paper I) · Topic 8.** 18/8 stainless steel contains (a) 18% Nickel, 8% Chromium (b) 18% Chromium, 8% Nickel (c) 18% Tungsten, 8% Nickel (d) 18% Tungsten, 8% Chromium

*Answer: (b) 18% chromium, 8% nickel.*

18/8 is the common austenitic stainless steel. The 18% chromium forms a thin, self-healing chromium oxide film that stops rusting; any steel with more than about 12% chromium is stainless. The 8% nickel keeps the steel austenitic at room temperature, so it is tough, ductile and non-magnetic.

- **(a):** the two numbers are swapped.
- **(c) and (d):** 18% tungsten belongs to 18-4-1 high-speed steel, not to stainless steel.

**The trap:** there are two famous 18s. In 18/8 stainless the 18 is chromium; in 18-4-1 HSS it is tungsten.

**Q5. Kerala PSC AE (Mechanical, Ports), 15 July 2026 · Topic 5.** Steel is an alloy of iron and carbon with carbon content varying up to (a) 0.5% (b) 1% (c) 1.5% (d) 2%

*Answer: (d) 2%.*

The line between steel and cast iron is the most carbon austenite can dissolve: 2.11% at 1147 °C, written as 2% in most papers. Up to that, the alloy can be heated to a single phase, austenite, so it can be forged and hardened. Beyond it, some liquid always freezes as ledeburite, and the alloy is cast iron.

- **(a) 0.5%:** the middle of the medium-carbon range.
- **(b) 1% and (c) 1.5%:** tool steels reach about 1.5%, but the diagram lets steel go further.

**The trap:** (c) is about the highest carbon in steels that are actually made. The question asks for the limit on the iron-carbon diagram.

**Q6. DFCCIL Executive (Mechanical), 28 September 2021 (Shift 1) · Topic 9.** Which of the following materials is/are used as matrix in fibre reinforced plastics? I. E-Glass II. Epoxy III. Kevlar 49 (a) Only II (b) Only II and III (c) Only I and II (d) Only III

*Answer: (a) Only II.*

In a fibre-reinforced plastic the matrix is the plastic itself: a resin such as epoxy, polyester or vinyl ester. It binds the fibres together, passes the load to them and protects them. E-glass and Kevlar 49 are the reinforcing fibres that carry the load.

- **(b) and (d):** Kevlar 49 is an aramid fibre, a reinforcement.
- **(c):** E-glass is the most common glass fibre, also a reinforcement.

**The trap:** all three materials are found in FRP, but only one of them is the matrix.

**Q7. RPSC Lecturer (Technical Education), Mechanical Paper I, 2020 · Topic 1.** Match List-I (Material) with List-II (Structure): A. Charcoal, B. Graphite, C. Chromium, D. Copper; 1. FCC, 2. HCP, 3. Amorphous, 4. BCC (a) A-2, B-3, C-1, D-4 (b) A-3, B-2, C-1, D-4 (c) A-3, B-2, C-4, D-1 (d) A-2, B-3, C-4, D-1

*Answer: (c) A-3, B-2, C-4, D-1.*

Charcoal is amorphous carbon. Graphite is carbon in hexagonal layers; it is not truly close packed, but it is the only hexagonal entry. Chromium is BCC and copper is FCC.

- **(a) and (d):** charcoal and graphite are swapped.
- **(b):** chromium and copper are swapped.

**Memory aid:** settle the two metals first. Copper FCC and chromium BCC give C-4 and D-1, and only (c) has both.

**Q8. ISRO Scientist/Engineer (Mechanical), 2017 · Topic 5.** The crystal structure of cementite is \_\_\_\_\_ (a) FCC (b) BCC (c) Tetragonal (d) Orthorhombic

*Answer: (d) Orthorhombic.*

Cementite, Fe₃C, is a compound with 6.67% carbon. Its orthorhombic cell holds 12 iron atoms and 4 carbon atoms. It is the hard, brittle phase in steel and white cast iron.

- **(a) FCC:** austenite.
- **(b) BCC:** ferrite.
- **(c) Tetragonal:** martensite is body-centred tetragonal.

**Memory aid:** four phases, four cells: ferrite BCC, austenite FCC, martensite BCT and cementite orthorhombic.

**Q9. UPSSSC Assistant Boring Technician, 3 July 2022 · Topic 9.** Brass is an alloy of : (a) copper and tin (b) copper and nickel (c) copper and iron (d) copper and zinc

*Answer: (d) Copper and zinc.*

Brass is copper with zinc. Cartridge brass (70% copper, 30% zinc) is the best for deep drawing, and Muntz metal (60/40) is worked hot.

- **(a) Copper and tin:** bronze.
- **(b) Copper and nickel:** cupronickel, used for coins and condenser tubes. With nickel in the majority it becomes Monel.
- **(c) Copper and iron:** not a standard alloy family.

**Memory aid:** brass has zinc, bronze has tin.

**Q10. RRB ALP CBT 2 (Fitter), 21 January 2019 (Shift 1) · Topic 7.** \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ is a process of case hardening in which the outer layer is made hard by increasing amount of carbon. (a) Annealing (b) Normalizing (c) Carburizing (d) Tempering

*Answer: (c) Carburizing.*

A low-carbon steel part is heated to about 900–950 °C in a carbon-rich medium: a pack of charcoal, a salt bath or a gas. Carbon diffuses into the surface and raises it to about 0.8–1% C. A quench then turns that case to hard martensite while the low-carbon core stays tough.

- **(a) Annealing, (b) Normalizing and (d) Tempering:** they treat the whole part and add nothing to its surface.

**Memory aid:** the name says what goes in: carburising adds carbon, nitriding adds nitrogen.

**Q11. UPRVUNL AE (Electronics), 2014 · Topic 1.** Miller indices are same for: (a) Crystal planes (b) Parallel planes (c) Perpendicular planes (d) Three crystallographic planes.

*Answer: (b) Parallel planes.*

Miller indices are the reciprocals of a plane's intercepts on the three axes, cleared of fractions. Parallel planes have intercepts in the same ratio, so they reduce to the same (hkl). That is why one set of indices stands for a whole family of parallel planes.

- **(a) Crystal planes:** too general. Different planes have different indices.
- **(c) Perpendicular planes:** (100) and (010) in a cubic crystal are perpendicular and have different indices.
- **(d) Three crystallographic planes:** has no meaning here.

**Memory aid:** a Miller index names a set of parallel planes, not one plane.

**Q12. ISRO IPRC Technical Assistant (Mechanical), 21 April 2016 · Topic 10.** A simple method of detecting surface flaws in welds is (a) Dye penetrant test (b) X-ray inspection (c) Impact test (d) Bend test

*Answer: (a) Dye penetrant test.*

The surface is cleaned and a coloured or fluorescent dye is applied. It seeps into cracks by capillary action. The surface dye is wiped off and a white developer draws the dye back out, so each crack shows as a red line. It is cheap, portable and works on any non-porous material, but only on flaws that are open to the surface.

- **(b) X-ray inspection:** finds internal flaws, but needs costly equipment and radiation safety, so it is not simple.
- **(c) Impact test and (d) Bend test:** destructive tests on samples. They cannot inspect a finished weld.

**The trap:** X-rays can show a surface crack too, but the stem asks for a simple method.

**Q13. TSPSC AEE (Mechanical) Paper II, 2015 · Topic 4.** The number of phases present in equilibrium at eutectic point (a) 0 (b) 1 (c) 2 (d) 3

*Answer: (d) 3.*

At the eutectic point, liquid ⇌ α + β: the liquid and two solids are in balance. The phase rule at fixed pressure gives F = C − P + 1 = 2 − 3 + 1 = 0, so the point is invariant: its temperature and all three compositions are fixed.

- **(c) 2:** just above the eutectic temperature (liquid and one solid) or just below it (α and β).
- **(b) 1:** inside a single-phase field.
- **(a) 0:** never possible.

**The trap:** the products, α + β, are two phases. At the eutectic point you must count the liquid as well.

**Q14. SSC JE Mechanical, 27 October 2020 (Evening) · Topic 8.** Tool steel contains carbon in the range of: (a) 0.5 to 1.5 (b) 0.3 to 0.6 (c) 0.2 to 0.9 (d) 0.1 to 0.3

*Answer: (a) 0.5 to 1.5 (per cent).*

A tool must be hard and resist wear, so it needs a lot of carbon to form martensite and carbides. Plain carbon tool steels run from about 0.5–0.6% up to 1.5% C.

- **(d) 0.1 to 0.3:** mild steel, for structures and sheet.
- **(b) 0.3 to 0.6:** medium-carbon steel, for shafts, axles and gears.
- **(c) 0.2 to 0.9:** runs from mild steel into high-carbon steel and stops short of the hardest tool steels.

**Memory aid:** the more a part must cut or resist wear, the more carbon it needs.

**Q15. ISRO Scientist/Engineer (Mechanical), 2020 · Topic 1.** The number of atoms per unit cell for an FCC crystal structure is (a) 2 (b) 4 (c) 6 (d) 8

*Answer: (b) 4.*

Each of the 8 corner atoms is shared by 8 cells, and each of the 6 face atoms by 2 cells: 8 × 1/8 + 6 × 1/2 = 1 + 3 = 4.

- **(a) 2:** BCC, 8 × 1/8 + 1.
- **(c) 6:** the hexagonal HCP cell, 12 × 1/6 + 2 × 1/2 + 3.
- **(d) 8:** counts each corner atom as a whole atom.

**Memory aid:** SC 1, BCC 2, FCC 4, HCP 6.

**Q16. Kerala PSC AE (Mechanical, Ports), 15 July 2026 · Topic 8.** All iron and steel products are derived originally from (a) Pig iron (b) Cast iron (c) Wrought iron (d) White cast iron

*Answer: (a) Pig iron.*

The blast furnace reduces iron ore with coke and limestone, and its product is pig iron, with about 3.5–4.5% carbon and some silicon, manganese, sulphur and phosphorus. Every other iron is made from it: remelted in a cupola it becomes cast iron, refined of almost all its carbon it becomes wrought iron, and refined to a set carbon content it becomes steel.

- **(b) Cast iron, (c) Wrought iron and (d) White cast iron:** all are made from pig iron.

**Memory aid:** ore → pig iron → everything else.

**Q17. APPSC Lecturer (Polytechnic Colleges), March 2020 · Topic 2.** Under the steady-state condition, Fick's first law is given as J = −D dc/dx, where J = Diffusion flux, D = Diffusion coefficient, dc/dx = Concentration gradient. The unit of diffusion coefficient will be: (a) m/s² (b) m/s (c) m²/s (d) kg/m³

*Answer: (c) m²/s.*

J is in kg/(m²·s) and dc/dx in (kg/m³)/m = kg/m⁴. So D = J ÷ (dc/dx) = kg/(m²·s) × m⁴/kg = m²/s.

- **(a) m/s²:** an acceleration.
- **(b) m/s:** a speed.
- **(d) kg/m³:** a concentration or a density.

**Memory aid:** every diffusivity is in m²/s: mass diffusivity, thermal diffusivity and kinematic viscosity alike.

**Q18. UKPSC JE Mechanical 2013 (Paper I) · Topic 7.** Which of the following is a case hardening process ? (a) Carburizing (b) Nitriding (c) Cyaniding (d) All of the above

*Answer: (d) All of the above.*

All three harden only a thin surface layer by adding an element to it: carburizing adds carbon, nitriding adds nitrogen and cyaniding adds both. The core keeps its toughness. Carbonitriding, and flame and induction hardening, are case hardening processes too.

- **(a), (b) and (c):** each is correct, but none alone.

**The trap:** before marking "all of the above", check each option on its own. Here each one is a case hardening process.

**Q19. BPSC AE (Mechanical) Paper 6, 2019 · Topic 6.** The resultant microstructure after normalizing should be (a) martensitic (b) pearlitic (c) bainitic (d) ferritic

*Answer: (b) Pearlitic.*

Normalizing heats steel 30–50 °C above the upper critical temperature and cools it in still air. That is faster than furnace cooling but far slower than a quench, so the austenite turns into fine pearlite, with some ferrite or cementite first depending on the carbon.

- **(a) Martensitic:** needs a quench faster than the critical cooling rate.
- **(c) Bainitic:** needs a hold at about 250–550 °C, as in austempering.
- **(d) Ferritic:** a low-carbon steel is mostly ferrite after normalizing, but that ferrite forms before the transformation. The product of the transformation itself is pearlite.

**Memory aid:** annealing gives coarse pearlite, normalizing fine pearlite, a quench martensite and austempering bainite.

**Q20. GATE PI 2020 · Topic 5.** Group I lists phases of steel and Group II lists crystal structures. Group I: P. Ferrite, Q. Austenite, R. Martensite. Group II: 1. HCP, 2. BCC, 3. BCT, 4. FCC. Match the phase with the corresponding crystal structure. (a) P-2, Q-4, R-3 (b) P-4, Q-2, R-3 (c) P-2, Q-4, R-1 (d) P-4, Q-2, R-1

*Answer: (a) P-2, Q-4, R-3.*

Ferrite is α-iron, BCC. Austenite is γ-iron, FCC. Martensite is body-centred tetragonal: the carbon trapped in it by the quench stretches the BCC cell along one axis.

- **(b) and (d):** ferrite and austenite are swapped.
- **(c) and (d):** no phase of steel is HCP.

**Memory aid:** martensite is the one with the T, for tetragonal.

**Q21. NRL Graduate Engineer Trainee (Mechanical), 23 September 2021 (Shift 2) · Topic 6.** Which of the following is NOT the type of annealing? (a) Full annealing (b) Process annealing (c) Spheroidise annealing (d) Flame annealing

*Answer: (d) Flame annealing.*

Flame belongs to flame hardening, a surface hardening process: an oxy-acetylene flame heats the surface and a water spray quenches it. The other three are real annealing treatments.

- **(a) Full annealing:** heat above the upper critical temperature and cool in the furnace. It gives the softest steel.
- **(b) Process annealing:** heat to about 550–650 °C, below the lower critical temperature, to restore ductility between cold-working stages.
- **(c) Spheroidise annealing:** a long hold just below 727 °C turns the cementite into small globules, so high-carbon steel machines easily.

**The trap:** "flame" is borrowed from flame hardening. The other annealing types are diffusion (homogenising), isothermal and stress-relief annealing.

**Q22. UKPSC JE Mechanical 2013 (Paper I) · Topic 3.** The process of formation of new grains on heating metals is called (a) Recrystallization (b) Oxidation (c) Microstructure (d) Hardening

*Answer: (a) Recrystallization.*

When a cold-worked metal is heated, it goes through three stages. In recovery, internal stresses fall as dislocations rearrange. In recrystallization, at about 0.4 times the melting point in kelvin, new strain-free grains form and the work hardening disappears. In grain growth, the new grains get bigger.

- **(b) Oxidation:** a reaction with oxygen.
- **(c) Microstructure:** describes the grains you see under a microscope. It is not a process.
- **(d) Hardening:** heating and quenching to make martensite.

**Memory aid:** recovery, recrystallization, grain growth, in that order as the temperature rises.

**Q17. Kerala PSC AE (Mechanical) / Inspector of Factories, 22 July 2026 · Topic 4.** In Hume-Rothery rules for extensive solid solubility, the atomic diameter of the solute and the solvent atoms should not differ by more than (a) 50% (b) 15% (c) 2% (d) 0%

(a) 1, 2 and 3 (b) 1, 2 and 4 (c) 1, 3 and 4 (d) 2, 3 and 4

*Answer: (c) 1, 3 and 4.*

Statement 1 is the textbook definition of a composite: a multiphase material that keeps a good share of the properties of each part. Statement 3 is its usual make-up, a matrix holding a reinforcement. Statement 4 is true: epoxy is the matrix of most glass and carbon fibre composites ("composition" here means composite). Statement 2 is false: the parts must be chemically dissimilar, with a clear boundary between them; otherwise the material is simply an alloy.

- **(a), (b) and (d):** each includes statement 2.

**Memory aid:** a composite is two different materials that stay different.

**Q18. HPCL Assistant Maintenance Technician (Mechanical), 2019 · Topic 3.** Cold working of metal forming processes is carried out \_\_\_\_\_\_. (a) below the recrystallising temperature (b) above the recrystallising temperature (c) above the melting point (d) below the melting point

*Answer: (a) Below the recrystallising temperature.*

Cold working is defined as working below the recrystallisation temperature, about 0.4 times the melting point in kelvin. The deformed grains are not replaced, so the metal work hardens.

- **(b) Above the recrystallising temperature:** hot working.
- **(c) Above the melting point:** the metal would be liquid, which is casting.
- **(d) Below the melting point:** true of every forming process, hot or cold, so it does not describe cold working.

**The trap:** (d) is a true statement but says nothing about cold working. The defining temperature is the recrystallisation temperature.

**Q19. UPMRC Fitter, 12 May 2018 · Topic 8.** Which of the following metals is the purest form of iron? (a) Cast iron (b) Wrought iron (c) Grey cast iron (d) White cast iron

*Answer: (b) Wrought iron.*

Wrought iron is more than 99% iron, with under 0.1% carbon and 1–3% of fibrous slag. It is soft, ductile and easy to forge and weld, and was used for chains, hooks and railings.

- **(a) Cast iron, (c) Grey cast iron and (d) White cast iron:** all hold 2–4% carbon, plus silicon and other elements.

**Memory aid:** wrought iron is nearly pure iron; pig iron is the least pure.

**Q20. RRB JE CBT 2 (Mechanical), 30 August 2019 (Shift 1) · Topic 5.** Hypoeutectoid steel consists of- (a) Primary ferrite and pearlite (b) Proeutectoid cementite, pearlite and transformed ledeburite (c) Primary cementite and pearlite (d) Pearlite

*Answer: (a) Primary ferrite and pearlite.*

A steel below 0.8% C, cooled slowly, starts throwing out ferrite at its upper critical temperature. The austenite left over gains carbon until it reaches 0.8% at 727 °C and turns into pearlite. The result is primary (proeutectoid) ferrite and pearlite, with more ferrite the lower the carbon.

- **(b):** a hypoeutectic white cast iron, between 2% and 4.3% C.
- **(c):** hypereutectoid steel.
- **(d):** eutectoid steel.

**Memory aid:** below 0.8% C the extra phase is ferrite; above it, cementite.

**Q21. NPCIL Stipendiary Trainee (Mechanical), 7 November 2019 (Shift 2) · Topic 8.** Which material has the best damping capacity? (a) High speed steel (b) Stainless steel (c) Mild steel (d) Cast iron

*Answer: (d) Cast iron.*

The graphite flakes in grey cast iron break up the metal and soak up vibration energy as heat. That is why lathe beds, machine bases and engine blocks are made of cast iron: they stay quiet and keep the machine steady.

- **(a) High speed steel, (b) Stainless steel and (c) Mild steel:** continuous metal with no flakes, so vibration passes through with little loss.

**Memory aid:** the flakes that make cast iron weak in tension make it good at damping.

**Q22. RRB JE CBT 2 (Mechanical), 29 August 2019 · Topic 7.** Cyaniding involves the addition of \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ for the hardening of surface. (a) Nichrome (b) Niobium (c) Neon (d) Nitrogen

*Answer: (d) Nitrogen.*

In cyaniding, the cyanide bath releases carbon and nitrogen together, and both diffuse into the steel's surface. Nitrogen is the only option that goes in.

- **(a) Nichrome:** a nickel-chromium alloy for heating elements.
- **(b) Niobium:** an alloying element for grain refinement, not a case hardening agent.
- **(c) Neon:** an inert gas that does not react with steel.

**Memory aid:** cyanide is CN, carbon and nitrogen.

**Q23. HPCL Junior Executive (Mechanical), 14 September 2025 (Shift 1) · Topic 1.** The common metals that crystallize in HCP structure are: (a) Magnesium, Titanium, Zinc (b) Iron (α), Tungsten, Molybdenum (c) Aluminium, Copper, Nickel (d) Silver, Gold, Platinum

*Answer: (a) Magnesium, titanium, zinc.*

Magnesium, titanium (below 882 °C), zinc, cadmium, cobalt and beryllium are HCP. With few slip systems at room temperature, they are less ductile than FCC metals.

- **(b):** all BCC.
- **(c) and (d):** all FCC.

**Q24. Kerala PSC AE (Mechanical, Ports), 15 July 2026 · Topic 7.** Identify surface hardening process from the following. (a) Process annealing (b) Isothermal quenching (c) Normalizing (d) Nitriding

*Answer: (d) Nitriding.*

Nitriding hardens only the surface: nitrogen from ammonia at about 500–550 °C forms hard nitrides in a thin case, with no quench needed.

- **(a) Process annealing:** softens the whole part between cold-working stages.
- **(b) Isothermal quenching:** austempering, which turns the whole section to bainite.
- **(c) Normalizing:** refines the grain of the whole part.

**Memory aid:** a surface process either adds an element to the surface or heats only the surface.

**Q25. ESE Mechanical 2020 · Topic 4.** In the study of phase diagrams, the rule which helps to calculate the relative proportions of liquid and solid material present in the mixture at any given temperature is known as (a) Hume-Rothery rule (b) Lever rule (c) Gibb's phase rule (d) Empirical rule

*Answer: (b) Lever rule.*

In a two-phase field, draw the tie line at the given temperature. The fraction of each phase is the length of the arm on the opposite side divided by the whole tie line, just as weights balance on a lever about a pivot at the alloy's composition.

- **(a) Hume-Rothery rule:** tells whether two metals dissolve in each other.
- **(c) Gibbs' phase rule:** gives the number of phases and degrees of freedom, not their amounts.
- **(d) Empirical rule:** not a named rule of phase diagrams.

**Memory aid:** tie line for the compositions, lever rule for the amounts.
