# Industrial Engineering Study Guide

## How to use this guide

Industrial Engineering is the fifth-ranked subject in your topic map, at about 10.7% of technical questions across RRB JE, SSC JE and Kerala PSC AE: about 9 of every 100 in RRB JE, 11 in SSC JE and 12 in Kerala PSC AE. Most of its questions are definitions, names and short sums (EOQ, standard time, critical path, break-even point), so it repays study time quickly. Finish it in about 13 study days, one sub-topic at a time, in the order below.

Work study comes first (sub-topics 1 and 2): how a job should be done, and how long it should take. Inventory and PERT/CPM (3 and 4) hold most of the calculation questions. Sub-topics 5 to 7 run the shop floor: planning, layout and quality. The last three are the management side: costs, principles and forecasting. Kerala PSC asks more of the management side than the other two exams.

For each sub-topic you get three things: the main contents to learn, what each one means in plain words (with the formulas you must memorise), and real questions from past papers (mostly 2019–2026) with worked solutions. Questions are tagged with the exam and year they appeared in.

## 1. Method study

Method study asks how a job should be done, and nearly all its questions are recall: the steps in order, the five process chart symbols, which chart records what, and the therbligs. Learn the chart rows of the table below by heart; they answer most questions.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Work study | Method study plus work measurement. Method study finds a better way to do a job and cuts its work content. Work measurement (sub-topic 2) then sets the standard time for the improved method. Taylor began time study; Frank and Lillian Gilbreth began motion study. |
| Steps of method study | Select the job, Record the present method, Examine it critically, Develop a better method, Install it, Maintain it (SREDIM). Some books add Define (write down the new method) after Develop. |
| Questioning technique | In the Examine step, every activity is asked: What is done (purpose)? Where (place)? When (sequence)? Who (person)? How (means)? Each answer leads to one of four actions: eliminate, combine, rearrange or simplify. |
| Process chart symbols | Five symbols (ASME): operation ○ (circle), inspection □ (square), transport → (arrow), delay D (temporary storage), storage ▽ (triangle, permanent storage). A circle inside a square is a combined operation and inspection. |
| Outline process chart | Also called the operation process chart. It shows only the main operations and inspections, with no transport, delay or storage. It gives a bird's-eye view of the whole process. |
| Flow process chart | Shows every step with all five symbols, plus distances and times. It is drawn for the man (what the worker does), the material (what happens to the part) or the equipment. It exposes non-productive transport, delays and storage. |
| Two-handed process chart | Records what the left hand and the right hand do, side by side. Used for short, repetitive bench work. |
| Multiple activity chart | Shows several workers or machines on one common time scale, so idle time shows up clearly. The man-machine chart is its best-known form. |
| SIMO chart | Simultaneous motion cycle chart. It records the therbligs of the different body parts (left hand, right hand, eyes, legs) on one time scale, usually from film. It belongs to micromotion study. |
| Flow diagram | The flow process chart drawn on the floor plan, so the path of men or material can be seen. |
| String diagram | A scale plan on which a string is run between work points to measure how far a worker or material travels. It shows long, repeated or backtracking movements. |
| Travel chart | A from–to table of the number of trips between work centres. Used when many items move between many centres. |
| Cyclegraph and chronocyclegraph | A small light is fixed to the worker's finger and photographed. A cyclegraph shows the path of the motion. A chronocyclegraph uses a flashing light, so the dots also show speed and direction. |
| Micromotion and memomotion study | Micromotion study films a short, highly repetitive job at normal speed and studies it frame by frame in therbligs (Gilbreth). Memomotion study films a long job at a slow frame rate, such as one frame per second. |
| Therbligs | Gilbreth's name spelt backwards: the 17 basic hand motions (18 in books that add "find"). Effective therbligs move the work forward (reach, move, grasp, release, use, assemble, disassemble, pre-position). Ineffective ones should be cut (search, select, position, hold, inspect, plan, unavoidable delay, avoidable delay, rest). |
| Principles of motion economy | Rules for the use of the human body, the arrangement of the workplace and the design of tools. Both hands should start and finish together, and move in opposite, symmetrical directions. Tools should have fixed places, within easy reach, and parts should be fed by gravity. Use the lowest class of movement that does the job. |
| Classes of body movement | Class 1 fingers (pivot: knuckle), class 2 fingers and wrist (wrist), class 3 forearm (elbow), class 4 upper arm (shoulder), class 5 body (torso). The higher the class, the more effort and time it takes. |

### Symbols and steps to memorise

- Steps: Select → Record → Examine → Develop → Install → Maintain (SREDIM)
- Symbols: operation ○, inspection □, transport →, delay D, storage ▽
- Which chart records what: outline chart (operations and inspections), flow process chart (all five symbols), two-handed chart (left and right hand), multiple activity chart (men and machines on one time scale), SIMO chart (therbligs of each body part on one time scale)
- Pivots of the five classes of movement: knuckle, wrist, elbow, shoulder, torso

### Past exam questions with solutions

**Q1. VIZAG MT Mechanical 2017.** Work study is concerned with: (a) improving present method and finding standard time (b) motivation of workers (c) improving production capability (d) all of the above

*Answer: (a).* Work study has two halves: method study improves the present method, and work measurement finds the standard time. Motivating workers belongs to incentive schemes, not work study. Option (d) is the trap: work study may raise output, but that is a result, not what it studies.

**Q2. UPRVUNL JE ME, 24 October 2021 (Shift 2).** The correct sequence of steps involved in method study is: (a) select, record, examine, develop, install, maintain (b) select, record, install, examine, develop, maintain (c) record, select, examine, install, develop, maintain (d) select, examine, record, develop, install, maintain

*Answer: (a).* Remember SREDIM. You must select a job before you can record it, and record it before you can examine it. A new method is installed only after it has been developed, and maintained after it is installed.

**Q3. BDL MT Mechanical, 13 April 2025 (Shift 2).** In process chart symbols, the circle represents: (a) storage (b) transport (c) delay (d) operation

*Answer: (d).* The circle is an operation, the only step that adds value to the part. Storage is a triangle, transport an arrow and delay a capital D.

**Q4. HPCL Junior Executive (Mechanical), 16 August 2025 (Shift 1).** What is the primary purpose of using a flow process chart in method study? (a) To design new machinery for production (b) To record and analyse the sequence of activities in a process (c) To calculate the cost of production (d) To train workers on new technology

*Answer: (b).* A flow process chart records every operation, inspection, transport, delay and storage in order, with distances and times. Laid out like this, the steps that add no value are easy to spot and remove.

**Q5. HPCL Engineer (Mechanical), 27 March 2025 (Shift 2).** A man-machine chart is a special type of: (a) multiple activity chart (b) flow process chart (c) string diagram (d) chronocycle graph

*Answer: (a).* A multiple activity chart puts several workers or machines on one time scale. The man-machine chart is the case of one worker and the machines they run, and it shows the idle time of each.

**Q6. Kerala PSC Deputy Engineer / AE (Mechanical), 18 June 2023.** A SIMO chart is used for studying the relationship of: (a) operator and machines (b) operator and materials (c) different limbs of an operator (d) time and motion of operators

*Answer: (c).* A SIMO chart records the therbligs of each body part (left hand, right hand, eyes) side by side on one time scale. Option (a) describes the man-machine chart.

**Q7. BPSC AE Paper 6 (Mechanical) 2012.** Therbligs consist of: (a) 15 basic hand motions (b) 16 basic hand motions (c) 17 basic hand motions (d) 20 basic hand motions

*Answer: (c).* Gilbreth's original list has 17 therbligs. Some books add "find" to make 18. If both 17 and 18 are offered, go with 17, as this key does.

**Q8. GATE PI 2015.** As per the principles of motion economy, which one of the following is NOT a pivot for a classified movement of the human body? (a) knee (b) elbow (c) torso (d) wrist

*Answer: (a).* The five classes of movement turn about the knuckle, wrist, elbow, shoulder and torso. The knee is not one of them. Leg and foot movements are planned separately, for example with foot pedals.

## 2. Work measurement and time study

Work measurement asks how long a job should take, and it gives the subject's most reliable sum: observed time × rating = normal time, then normal time + allowances = standard time. Learn which way the allowance is added (on normal time or on standard time), because that is where the options differ.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Work measurement | Finding the time a qualified worker needs to do a specified job at a defined level of performance. It follows method study, so the time is set for the improved method. |
| Qualified worker and standard rating | A qualified worker has the skill, knowledge and fitness to do the job to the required quality. Standard rating (100 on the BSI scale) is the pace such a worker keeps up through the day without overstrain. |
| Stopwatch time study | The job is split into elements, each element is timed over several cycles, the times are rated and allowances are added. Used for short, repetitive jobs. In the continuous method the watch runs throughout; in the snap-back (fly-back) method it is reset to zero after each element. |
| Performance rating | The observer compares the worker's pace with standard pace: 120% means faster than standard, 80% slower. Methods include speed rating, the Westinghouse system (skill, effort, conditions, consistency) and synthetic rating (comparing with PMTS times). |
| Normal time | Observed time corrected to standard pace: observed time × rating ÷ 100. A fast worker's observed time is raised; a slow worker's is cut. |
| Allowances | Time added to normal time for things that are not part of the job itself: personal needs, fatigue (rest), unavoidable delays, contingencies, and policy allowances. |
| Standard time | Normal time plus allowances. It is the time used to plan production, set costs and pay incentives. |
| Work sampling | Also called ratio delay study. Many observations are taken at random instants, and the share of observations in which a worker or machine is busy estimates the share of time it is busy. Used for long or non-repetitive jobs, machine utilisation and setting allowances. Its error falls as 1/√N. |
| PMTS | Predetermined motion time systems give times for basic motions (reach, move, grasp) from tables, so no stopwatch or rating is needed. Examples: MTM (methods-time measurement), Work-Factor and MOST. MTM times are in TMU: 1 TMU = 0.00001 hour = 0.036 second. |
| Synthetic data and estimating | Synthetic data builds the time for a new job from element times found in earlier studies. Analytical estimating is used for long, non-repetitive jobs such as maintenance work. |
| Operator efficiency | Standard time for the work done ÷ time actually taken × 100. Above 100% means the operator beat the standard. |

### Formulas to memorise

```math
\text{Normal time} = \text{Observed time} \times \frac{\text{Rating}}{100}
```

```math
\text{Standard time} = NT\,(1 + A) \quad \text{(allowance } A \text{ as a fraction of normal time)}
```

```math
\text{Standard time} = \frac{NT}{1 - A} \quad \text{(allowance } A \text{ as a fraction of standard time)}
```

```math
\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Standard time for work done}}{\text{Actual time taken}} \times 100 \qquad \text{Work sampling error} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
```

### Past exam questions with solutions

**Q1. HPCL Junior Executive (Mechanical), 14 September 2025 (Shift 1).** Standard time is defined as: (a) normal time + allowances (b) normal time − allowances (c) observed time × performance rating (d) observed time + allowances

*Answer: (a).* Allowances are always added, never taken away, and they are added to normal time, not observed time. Option (c) gives normal time, which is one step short. Option (d) skips the rating.

**Q2. HPCL Engineer (Mechanical), 27 March 2025 (Shift 2).** The observed time for a worker is 0.7 minutes and his performance rating is 80%. The normal time taken by the worker is: (a) 0.7 minutes (b) 0.56 minutes (c) 0.875 minutes (d) 0.95 minutes

*Answer: (b).* Normal time = 0.7 × 80/100 = 0.56 min. A worker rated below 100% is slower than standard, so a standard worker would take less time than was observed. Option (c) divides by the rating instead of multiplying.

**Q3. UPPSC AE Mechanical 2013 (Paper I).** In a time study the observed time is 0.75 min, the performance rating factor is 110% and allowances are 20% of the normal time. The standard time is: (a) 0.82 min (b) 0.975 min (c) 0.99 min (d) 1.03 min

*Answer: (c).* Normal time = 0.75 × 1.10 = 0.825 min. The allowance is a share of normal time, so standard time = 0.825 × 1.20 = 0.99 min. Option (a) stops at normal time.

**Q4. RPSC Lecturer Technical Education 2020 (Mechanical, Paper I).** In a stopwatch time study, the observed time was 0.16 minutes and the performance rating factor was 125 on the 100-normal scale. The standard time, if 10% allowances are permitted, is: (a) 0.180 min (b) 0.200 min (c) 0.220 min (d) 0.240 min

*Answer: (c).* Normal time = 0.16 × 1.25 = 0.20 min, and standard time = 0.20 × 1.10 = 0.22 min. Option (b) is the normal time, the usual half-way trap.

**Q5. Kerala PSC AE (Mechanical) / Inspector of Factories, 22 July 2026.** A data entry operator completes his task in 300 minutes with a performance rating of 80%. The standard time for the task is 276 minutes. The allowance provided for the task is: (a) 20 percent (b) 8 percent (c) 15 percent (d) none of these

*Answer: (c).* Normal time = 300 × 0.80 = 240 min. Allowance = 276 − 240 = 36 min, and 36 ÷ 240 = 15% of normal time. Option (b) comes from comparing 276 with 300, the observed time, which skips the rating.

**Q6. ISRO VSSC Technical Assistant (Mechanical), 30 July 2023.** The standard time allowed for a job is 10 minutes per piece for production and 50 minutes as setting time. An operator assembles 50 jobs in 8 hours and 20 minutes, including the time for setting his machine. The operator's efficiency is: (a) 100% (b) 110% (c) 90.9% (d) 90%

*Answer: (b).* Standard time earned = 50 × 10 + 50 = 550 min. Time actually taken = 8 h 20 min = 500 min. Efficiency = 550/500 × 100 = 110%. Option (c) turns the ratio upside down.

**Q7. UPPSC AE Mechanical 2013 (Paper I).** The error estimate (e) in work sampling varies with the sample size (n) as: (a) e ∝ 1/n (b) e ∝ 1/√n (c) e ∝ √n (d) e ∝ 1/n²

*Answer: (b).* Work sampling estimates a proportion, and the standard error of a proportion is √(p(1 − p)/n). To halve the error you need four times as many observations, not twice as many.

**Q8. BPSC AE Paper 6 (Mechanical), 25 March 2022.** Which one of the following is not a technique under Predetermined Motion Time System (PMTS)? (a) Work factor (b) Synthetic data (c) Stopwatch time study (d) MTM

*Answer: (c).* A stopwatch time study times the worker directly and needs a rating, while PMTS takes motion times from tables. Work-Factor and MTM are true PMTS. Synthetic data also uses times found in advance, so the key groups it with them.

## 3. Inventory control

Inventory control gives one sure sum in nearly every paper, the economic order quantity, plus recall of the ABC family of classifications. Check the units of the holding cost before using it in the EOQ formula: a cost per month must be turned into a cost per year.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Inventory | Stock of raw materials, work in progress, finished goods and maintenance spares. It is held to keep production smooth, to buy in economical lots and as a cushion against late deliveries or sudden demand. |
| Ordering cost | The cost of placing and receiving one order: paperwork, follow-up, transport and receiving inspection. For an item made in-house it is the set-up cost. It does not depend on the order quantity, so fewer, larger orders cut the yearly ordering cost. |
| Carrying (holding) cost | The cost of keeping stock for a year: interest on the money locked up, storage space, insurance, taxes, depreciation, deterioration and obsolescence. It is often 15–25% of the item's value per year, and it rises with order size. |
| Shortage (stock-out) cost | Lost sales, lost goodwill, idle machines and costly emergency buying when the item runs out. |
| EOQ (Wilson's model) | The order size that makes the total of ordering and carrying cost a minimum. At the EOQ the yearly ordering cost equals the yearly carrying cost. |
| EOQ assumptions | Known, constant demand; constant lead time; the whole order arrives at once (instantaneous replenishment); no shortages; a fixed price with no quantity discounts; ordering cost per order does not change with quantity. |
| Economic batch quantity (EBQ) | For an item made in-house, stock builds up while the batch is being produced and used at the same time, so the formula carries an extra factor (1 − d/p), where d is the usage rate and p the production rate. The EBQ is larger than the EOQ. |
| Lead time | The time between placing an order and receiving the goods. |
| Reorder point (reorder level) | The stock level at which a new order is placed: demand during the lead time plus safety stock. |
| Safety (buffer) stock | Extra stock kept to cover demand and lead time that turn out higher than expected. More safety stock means fewer stock-outs but a higher carrying cost. |
| ABC analysis | Items are ranked by annual consumption value (yearly usage × unit cost), following Pareto's rule. A items: about 10% of items, 70% of value, controlled tightly. B: about 20% of items, 20% of value. C: about 70% of items, only 10% of value, controlled loosely. |
| Other selective controls | VED: vital, essential, desirable (how critical the item is to production; used for spares). FSN: fast, slow, non-moving (rate of issue). HML: high, medium, low unit price. SDE: scarce, difficult, easy to get. GOLF: government, ordinary, local, foreign source. XYZ: value of the stock in hand. |
| Inventory systems | Fixed order quantity (Q system): order the same quantity whenever stock falls to the reorder point. Periodic review (P system): check stock at fixed intervals and order enough to bring it up to a set level. The two-bin system is a simple Q system. A bin card records the receipts, issues and balance of an item. |

### Formulas to memorise

```math
Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2DC_o}{C_h}} \qquad TC_{\min} = \sqrt{2DC_oC_h}
```

```math
\text{Annual ordering cost} = \frac{D}{Q}\,C_o \qquad \text{Annual carrying cost} = \frac{Q}{2}\,C_h \qquad \text{Orders per year} = \frac{D}{Q}
```

```math
\text{Reorder point} = d \times L + \text{safety stock}
```

```math
Q_{\text{EBQ}} = \sqrt{\frac{2DC_o}{C_h\,(1 - d/p)}}
```

Here D is the annual demand, C\_o the cost per order, C\_h the holding cost per unit per year, d the demand per day and L the lead time in days.

### Past exam questions with solutions

**Q1. Kerala PSC Deputy Engineer / AE (Mechanical), 18 June 2023.** The selective inventory technique ABC gives importance to: (a) items which are rare (b) items which are critical (c) value of the items (d) demand for the items

*Answer: (c).* ABC ranks items by annual consumption value. Option (b) is VED analysis, which ranks by how critical an item is. Option (a) describes SDE, which ranks by how hard an item is to get.

**Q2. RRB JE CBT 2 (Mechanical and Allied Engineering), 2 July 2026.** In ABC analysis, the letter 'C' is designated to: (a) high valued items, smaller in number (b) high valued items, larger in number (c) low valued items, larger in number (d) low valued items, smaller in number

*Answer: (c).* C items are the many cheap ones, about 70% of the items but only about 10% of the money. Option (a) describes A items: few in number, most of the value.

**Q3. APPSC Lecturer (Polytechnic Colleges), March 2020.** Which one of the following is NOT a technique of inventory control? (a) ABC analysis (b) FSN analysis (c) GOLF analysis (d) FTMN analysis

*Answer: (d).* ABC (value), FSN (rate of use) and GOLF (source of supply) are all selective inventory controls. FTMN is not a recognised one. GOLF is the one people doubt, so learn it: government, ordinary, local, foreign.

**Q4. UPPSC AE Mains Mechanical 2024 (Paper I), 29 September 2025.** Which cost includes insurance, depreciation and property taxes? (a) Set-up cost (b) Inventory carrying cost (c) Stock-out cost (d) Ordering cost

*Answer: (b).* Insurance, taxes and depreciation are paid because stock is being held, and they grow with the amount held. That makes them carrying costs. Ordering and set-up costs are paid per order, whatever its size.

**Q5. UPPSC AE Mains Mechanical 2024 (Paper I), 29 September 2025.** At EOQ with a uniform rate of demand, which statement is correct? (a) Annual ordering cost = annual carrying cost (b) Annual shortage cost = annual purchase cost (c) Annual purchase cost = annual ordering cost (d) Annual carrying cost = annual shortage cost

*Answer: (a).* Ordering cost falls and carrying cost rises as the order size grows, and their total is lowest where the two curves cross. The basic EOQ model allows no shortages, so options (b) and (d) cannot apply.

**Q6. RRB JE CBT 2 (Mechanical and Allied Engineering), 2 July 2026.** A company faces an annual demand of 10,000 units, a fixed ordering cost of ₹200 per order, and a holding cost of ₹4 per unit per year. The EOQ is: (a) 4000 units (b) 2240 units (c) 1000 units (d) 5000 units

*Answer: (c).* EOQ = √(2 × 10,000 × 200 ÷ 4) = √1,000,000 = 1000 units. Quick check: 10 orders a year cost 10 × 200 = ₹2000, and an average stock of 500 costs 500 × 4 = ₹2000. The two are equal, as they must be.

**Q7. RRB JE ME CBT 2, 29 August 2019.** The demand rate for a particular item is 12000 units/year. The ordering cost is Rs. 100 per order and the holding cost is Rs. 0.80 per item per month. If no shortages are allowed and the replacement is instantaneous, the economic order quantity is: (a) 1500 units (b) 2000 units (c) 500 units (d) 1000 units

*Answer: (c).* First turn the holding cost into a yearly figure: 0.80 × 12 = Rs. 9.60 per unit per year. EOQ = √(2 × 12000 × 100 ÷ 9.6) = √250,000 = 500 units. Using 0.80 as it stands gives about 1732 units, which is not offered; the trap here is the monthly cost.

**Q8. UPPSC AE Mechanical 2022 (Paper I), 29 May 2022.** A company uses 2555 units of an item annually. The delivery lead time is 8 days. The reorder point, in number of units, is: (a) 7 (b) 60 (c) 8 (d) 56

*Answer: (d).* Daily use = 2555 ÷ 365 = 7 units. Reorder point = 7 × 8 = 56 units (no safety stock is given). Option (a) is the daily use, one step short.

## 4. PERT and CPM

PERT and CPM plan a project as a network of activities, and three questions come back in every paper: the expected time (t₀ + 4tₘ + tₚ)/6, the critical path, and float. Learn the differences between PERT and CPM as a pair; they are often asked as true-or-false statements.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Activity and event | An activity is a task that takes time and resources, drawn as an arrow. An event (node) is an instant, the start or finish of activities, and takes no time. |
| Dummy activity | A dashed arrow that takes no time and no resources. It shows that one activity depends on another, or keeps two activities from having the same start and end events. |
| Network rules | Each activity has its own pair of events, there are no loops, and every activity except the last must lead somewhere (no dangling arrows). Events are numbered by Fulkerson's rule, so every arrow runs from a smaller to a larger number. |
| CPM (critical path method) | Uses one, fixed time for each activity (deterministic), so it is activity-oriented. It also trades time against cost. Suited to repeated jobs with known times, such as construction and maintenance. |
| PERT (programme evaluation and review technique) | Uses three time estimates because the times are uncertain (probabilistic), and is event-oriented. Suited to new work such as research and development. It was developed for the US Navy's Polaris missile project. |
| Three time estimates | Optimistic time t₀ (shortest, if everything goes right), most likely time tₘ, pessimistic time tₚ (longest, if things go wrong). Each activity's time is assumed to follow a beta distribution. |
| Forward and backward pass | The forward pass from the start gives the earliest start (EST) and earliest finish (EFT) of each activity. The backward pass from the end gives the latest finish (LFT) and latest start (LST) that do not delay the project. |
| Critical path | The longest path through the network. Its length is the shortest time in which the whole project can be finished. Every activity on it has zero total float, so any delay on it delays the project. A network can have more than one critical path. |
| Total float | How long an activity can be delayed without delaying the project: LST − EST, or LFT − EFT. |
| Free and independent float | Free float is the delay that does not affect the earliest start of the next activity. Independent float is the delay that affects neither the activities before nor those after it. Total float ≥ free float ≥ independent float. |
| Slack | The float of an event: its latest time minus its earliest time. Critical events have the least slack (zero when the project's target date equals its earliest finish). |
| Project duration in PERT | The project time is the sum of the critical activities' expected times, and its variance is the sum of their variances. By the central limit theorem the project time follows a normal distribution, so the chance of finishing by the expected time is 50%. |
| Crashing | Shortening activities by spending more (overtime, extra machines). Crash the critical activity with the lowest cost slope first. The best project duration is where direct plus indirect cost is least. |

### Formulas to memorise

```math
t_e = \frac{t_0 + 4t_m + t_p}{6} \qquad \sigma = \frac{t_p - t_0}{6} \qquad \text{variance} = \sigma^2
```

```math
\text{Total float} = LFT - EST - t = LST - EST
```

```math
Z = \frac{T_s - T_e}{\sigma_{cp}} \qquad \sigma_{cp} = \sqrt{\sum \sigma^2 \text{ (critical activities)}}
```

```math
\text{Cost slope} = \frac{\text{Crash cost} - \text{Normal cost}}{\text{Normal time} - \text{Crash time}}
```

### Past exam questions with solutions

**Q1. JKSSB JE (Civil), 19 November 2023.** The expected time (E) of a PERT activity in terms of optimistic time (O), pessimistic time (P) and most likely time (M) is given by: (a) E = (O + 2M + P)/3 (b) E = (O + 4M + P)/3 (c) E = (O + 2M + P)/6 (d) E = (O + 4M + P)/6

*Answer: (d).* The most likely time gets weight 4 and the other two weight 1 each, so the total weight is 6. Quick check: put O = M = P = 1 and the right formula must give E = 1. Only (d) does.

**Q2. AAI JE (Technical) 2018.** For an activity, the pessimistic, most likely and optimistic times are 10 days, 6 days and 2 days respectively. The expected duration of the activity based on the beta distribution will be: (a) 6 days (b) 3 days (c) 2 days (d) 9 days

*Answer: (a).* tₑ = (2 + 4 × 6 + 10)/6 = 36/6 = 6 days. The question lists the times in the order pessimistic, most likely, optimistic, the reverse of the usual order, so read the labels and not the order.

**Q3. MPSC AE Civil Mains 2019 (Paper 1).** In PERT, with expected mean time tₘ, optimistic time t₀ and pessimistic time tₚ, the variance is given by: (a) (tₚ − t₀)/6 (b) (t₀ + 4tₘ + tₚ)/6 (c) (tₚ − t₀)² (d) ((tₚ − t₀)/6)²

*Answer: (d).* The standard deviation is (tₚ − t₀)/6, and the variance is its square. Option (a) is the standard deviation, which is the usual trap, and option (b) is the expected time.

**Q4. RRB JE ME CBT 2, 31 August 2019.** Which of the following distributions represents the time estimates in PERT? (a) Poisson distribution (b) Normal distribution (c) Beta distribution (d) Weibull distribution

*Answer: (c).* Each activity's time is taken to follow a beta distribution, which has fixed ends (t₀ and tₚ) and can lean either way. The normal distribution is used for the whole project's completion time, so do not mix the two.

**Q5. UPRVUNL AE ME, 5 July 2021.** The \_\_\_\_ path within a network diagram is called the critical path. (a) longest (b) shortest (c) easiest (d) curved

*Answer: (a).* The project cannot finish until its longest chain of activities is done, so the longest path sets the shortest possible project time. That is why it is critical. "Shortest" is the trap, because the critical path gives the shortest project duration.

**Q6. GPSC AE Civil 2021, 27 November 2021.** A \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ is similar to an activity but it does not consume any resources. (a) slack (b) event (c) dummy (d) none of the above

## 5. Production planning and control

Production planning and control (PPC) is mostly vocabulary: what routing, scheduling, loading and dispatching each mean, and the order they come in. Add Johnson's rule for sequencing, MRP's inputs and the basics of JIT, and you can answer nearly every question.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Types of production | Job production: one-off or small orders on general-purpose machines (a tool room). Batch production: lots of the same item at intervals. Mass (flow) production: large volumes of one item on special-purpose machines in a fixed sequence (car assembly). Continuous production: process industries such as oil refining. |
| Production planning and control | Planning decides what to make, how, where and when. Control then checks that work goes to plan and corrects it when it does not. |
| Routing | Fixes the path of the work: the operations, their sequence and the machines to use. It is recorded on a route sheet. Routing answers "what and where". |
| Scheduling | Fixes when each operation starts and finishes, from a master schedule down to daily shop schedules. Gantt charts (bars against a time scale) are its main tool. Scheduling answers "when". |
| Loading | Assigns jobs to work centres or machines according to their capacity, so no machine is overloaded or left idle. |
| Dispatching | Releases the work: it issues the work orders, move tickets, material and tool requisitions that authorise production to start as scheduled. It is the action step of PPC. |
| Follow-up (expediting) | Chases the progress of work, finds delays and bottlenecks and removes them. |
| Order of PPC steps | Routing → scheduling → dispatching → follow-up. Loading sits with scheduling. Memory aid: "Real Students Do Follow-up". |
| Sequencing rules | First come, first served (FCFS); shortest processing time (SPT), which gives the least average flow time; earliest due date (EDD), which cuts the maximum lateness; critical ratio (time left to due date ÷ work left), where a ratio below 1 means the job is behind. |
| Johnson's rule | Gives the order of n jobs on two machines, all in the same machine order, that finishes all of them in the least total time (makespan). |
| Aggregate planning | A medium-term (about 3 to 18 months) plan of total output and workforce for a family of products. A chase strategy changes output to follow demand; a level strategy keeps output steady and lets stock absorb the changes. |
| Master production schedule (MPS) | How many of each end product to make in each week. It drives MRP. |
| MRP (material requirements planning) | Works out how many of each part and raw material to order, and when, for the products in the MPS. Inputs: MPS, bill of materials (BOM) and inventory status file. Output: planned order releases. It handles dependent demand, the parts that go into the end product. MRP II (manufacturing resource planning) adds capacity, money and labour. |
| JIT (just in time) | A Toyota system that makes only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed. It is a pull system: each stage takes parts from the stage before only when it uses them, signalled by kanban cards. It needs small lots, quick set-ups, reliable suppliers and quality at source, and it aims to remove waste. |
| Productivity | Output ÷ input. Labour productivity is output per worker-hour; total factor productivity uses all inputs together. |

### Formulas and steps to memorise

```math
\text{Critical ratio} = \frac{\text{Due date} - \text{Today's date}}{\text{Processing time remaining}} \qquad \text{Productivity} = \frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Input}}
```

Johnson's rule for n jobs on two machines:

1. Find the smallest processing time among all jobs on both machines.
2. If it is on machine 1, put that job as early as possible; if it is on machine 2, put it as late as possible.
3. Strike out that job and repeat with the rest. Break a tie on the same machine either way.

## 6. Plant layout and material handling

Layout questions are mostly matching: which layout suits which kind of production. Tie each layout to volume and variety and the answers follow. Line balancing adds one short sum, the balance delay.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Plant layout | The arrangement of machines, work areas, stores and services inside the factory so that material flows smoothly, with the least handling, at the lowest cost. A good layout also uses space well, stays flexible and keeps people safe. |
| Product (line) layout | Machines stand in the order of the operations, so the product flows along a line. For high volume and low variety (mass production: car assembly, bottling). Handling, work in progress and scheduling are all simple. But it is inflexible, needs costly special machines, and one breakdown stops the whole line. |
| Process (functional) layout | Similar machines are grouped together: all lathes in one bay, all milling machines in another. For low volume and high variety (job and batch production). It is flexible and a breakdown stops only one machine, but material travels more, work in progress piles up and scheduling is harder. |
| Fixed position layout | The product stays in one place and the workers, tools and materials come to it. Used for very large or heavy products: ships, aircraft, locomotives, bridges. |
| Group (cellular) layout | Based on group technology: parts with similar shapes or processes are grouped into families, and each family is made in its own cell of machines. It gives some of the flow of a product layout with some of the flexibility of a process layout. Machine-part groups are found by methods such as rank order clustering. |
| Combination layout | A mix of the above, for example a process layout for parts feeding a product layout for final assembly. |
| Line balancing | Sharing the tasks of an assembly line among work stations so each station has nearly the same work time. The slowest station sets the cycle time, and the idle time at the other stations is lost. Methods include the largest candidate rule, the Kilbridge–Wester method, ranked positional weights and COMSOAL. |
| Material handling | Moving, storing and protecting materials inside the plant. It adds cost but no value, so the aim is to handle less, over shorter distances. |
| Principles of material handling | Unit load: move items in the largest practical unit (pallets, containers). Use gravity where you can. Keep flow in straight lines. Mechanise repeated moves. Standardise equipment, and never trade safety for speed. |
| Handling equipment | Conveyors run along a fixed path and move material continuously: belt (bulk material, long or inclined runs), roller, chain, screw, and bucket conveyors or elevators for lifting granular material. Cranes and hoists serve a fixed area and move loads intermittently (EOT, jib, gantry cranes). Industrial trucks follow any path (forklifts, pallet trucks, automated guided vehicles). |

### Formulas to memorise

```math
C = \frac{\text{Available time per shift}}{\text{Required output per shift}} \qquad n_{\min} = \frac{\sum t_i}{C}
```

```math
\text{Line efficiency} = \frac{\sum t_i}{n \times C} \times 100 \qquad \text{Balance delay} = \frac{nC - \sum t_i}{nC} \times 100
```

Here C is the cycle time, tᵢ the time of each task and n the number of stations. Balance delay = 100% − line efficiency.

## 7. Quality control

Quality control questions turn on one split: variables (measured, such as a diameter) use X̄ and R charts, while attributes (counted, good or bad) use p and c charts. Add the OC curve, 3σ limits and Six Sigma's 3.4 defects per million, and you have most of what is asked.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Quality | Fitness for use (Juran), or conformance to specifications (Crosby). |
| Statistical quality control (SQC) | Using samples and statistics instead of checking every piece. It has two parts: process control with control charts (Shewhart) while the product is being made, and product control with acceptance sampling of finished lots. |
| Chance and assignable causes | Chance (common) causes are many small, unavoidable variations that are always present. Assignable (special) causes are definite faults, such as a worn tool or a wrong setting, which can be found and removed. A process with only chance causes is "in control". |
| Control charts for variables | For a quality you measure. The X̄ chart tracks the process average (sample means), and the R chart tracks the spread (sample ranges). They are drawn as a pair. The σ chart is an alternative to the R chart. |
| Control charts for attributes | For a quality you count. p chart: fraction defective (based on the binomial distribution). np chart: number of defectives. c chart: number of defects per unit, with a fixed sample size (based on the Poisson distribution). u chart: defects per unit when the sample size varies. |
| 3σ limits | Control limits are set at the centre line ± 3 standard deviations, so 99.73% of points fall inside when only chance causes act. A point outside, or a long run on one side of the centre line, signals an assignable cause. |
| Acceptance sampling | A sample is drawn from a lot, and the whole lot is accepted or rejected on what the sample shows. Used when testing destroys the item, or when 100% inspection is too costly or tiring. Plans can be single, double, multiple or sequential. |
| OC curve | The operating characteristic curve plots the probability of accepting a lot against the lot's fraction defective. It shows how well a plan tells good lots from bad. A larger sample makes the curve steeper, which discriminates better. |
| AQL, LTPD and the two risks | AQL (acceptable quality level) is the worst quality still thought good. LTPD (lot tolerance per cent defective) is the quality thought bad. Producer's risk α is the chance of rejecting a good lot; consumer's risk β is the chance of accepting a bad one. |
| Process capability | How well a process can hold the tolerance: Cₚ = (USL − LSL)/6σ. Cₚ ≥ 1.33 is usually taken as capable. |
| TQM | Total quality management: quality is everyone's job, aimed at customer satisfaction through continuous improvement (kaizen). It uses Deming's PDCA cycle (plan, do, check, act) and quality circles. |
| Six Sigma | A Motorola method (1986) that aims at no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities. Projects follow DMAIC: define, measure, analyse, improve, control. |
| Seven QC tools | Check sheet, histogram, Pareto chart (the vital few causes behind most defects, the 80/20 rule), cause-and-effect (Ishikawa or fishbone) diagram, scatter diagram, control chart, and flowchart or stratification. A fishbone diagram groups causes under the 6Ms: man, machine, material, method, measurement and mother nature (environment). |
| ISO 9000 | The international family of standards for quality management systems. ISO 14000 covers environmental management. |
| Bathtub curve | Failure rate over a product's life: high and falling at first (infant mortality, from manufacturing defects), then low and constant (useful life, random failures), then rising (wear-out). |

### Formulas to memorise

```math
\bar{X}\text{ chart: } \bar{\bar{X}} \pm A_2\bar{R} \qquad R\text{ chart: } UCL = D_4\bar{R},\; LCL = D_3\bar{R}
```

```math
p\text{ chart: } \bar{p} \pm 3\sqrt{\frac{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})}{n}} \qquad c\text{ chart: } \bar{c} \pm 3\sqrt{\bar{c}}
```

```math
C_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\sigma}
```

## 8. Break-even analysis and depreciation

Break-even questions are one formula: break-even quantity = fixed cost ÷ (selling price − variable cost per unit). The rest is vocabulary (contribution, P/V ratio, margin of safety) and the main depreciation methods.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed cost | Cost that does not change with output in the short run: rent, salaries, insurance, interest, depreciation of plant. |
| Variable cost | Cost that rises in step with output: raw material, direct labour, power for the machines. Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost per unit × quantity. |
| Contribution | Selling price − variable cost, per unit or in total. It first pays off the fixed cost; whatever is left is profit. |
| Break-even point (BEP) | The output at which sales revenue equals total cost, so there is neither profit nor loss. At this point the total contribution exactly equals the fixed cost. |
| P/V ratio | Profit-volume ratio = contribution ÷ sales. The higher it is, the faster profit grows with sales. |
| Margin of safety | Actual (or planned) sales minus break-even sales. It shows how far sales can fall before the firm makes a loss. |
| Break-even chart | Cost and revenue (y-axis) plotted against volume of output (x-axis). The sales line crosses the total cost line at the BEP. The angle between them is the angle of incidence; a larger angle means profit builds up faster. |
| What moves the BEP | A higher fixed cost or variable cost raises the BEP; a higher selling price lowers it. The number of units actually sold does not move the BEP; it only decides the profit or loss. |
| Make or buy | Making costs F + vQ; buying costs pQ. The two are equal at Q = F/(p − v). Above that quantity, making in-house is cheaper. |
| Depreciation | The fall in an asset's value through wear, age and obsolescence, charged as a yearly cost. Book value = original cost − depreciation charged so far. |
| Straight-line method | The same charge every year: (cost − scrap value) ÷ life. Simple, and book value falls in a straight line. |
| Declining balance method | Also called written-down value or reducing balance. A fixed percentage of the current book value is charged each year, so the charge is largest in the early years. The book value never reaches zero. |
| Sum-of-years'-digits method | For a life of n years, add the digits 1 + 2 + … + n. Year k's charge is (n − k + 1) ÷ that sum × (cost − scrap). Like the declining balance method, it charges more in the early years. |
| Sinking fund method | An equal sum is set aside each year and earns interest, so that the fund equals cost − scrap value at the end of the life. |

### Formulas to memorise

```math
Q_{BEP} = \frac{F}{s - v} \qquad \text{BEP sales (Rs.)} = \frac{F}{1 - v/s} = \frac{F}{P/V}
```

```math
P/V = \frac{s - v}{s} \qquad \text{Margin of safety} = \text{Actual sales} - \text{BEP sales} = \frac{\text{Profit}}{P/V}
```

```math
D_{SL} = \frac{C - S}{n} \qquad D_k^{SOYD} = \frac{n - k + 1}{n(n+1)/2}\,(C - S) \qquad BV_k^{DB} = C\,(1 - f)^k
```

Here F is the fixed cost, s the selling price and v the variable cost per unit, C the original cost, S the scrap value, n the life in years and f the declining-balance rate.

Annual depreciation = (original cost − scrap value)/life in years. (a) straight line method (b) declining balance method (c) sinking fund method (d) quantity survey method

*Answer: (a).* Dividing the total loss of value equally over the life gives the same charge every year, which is the straight-line method. The declining balance method uses a fixed percentage of book value, and the sinking fund method allows for interest.

## 9. Management principles and forms of ownership

This is Kerala PSC's favourite corner of the subject: Taylor and Fayol, the types of organisation, and the forms of business ownership. The other exams ask it less often. Learn the organisation types as a set, by who holds authority in each.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Functions of management | Fayol: planning, organising, commanding (directing), coordinating and controlling. Controlling means checking progress against the plan and correcting it. Gulick's POSDCORB adds staffing, reporting and budgeting. |
| Taylor's scientific management | F. W. Taylor (1911) replaced rule of thumb with science. His principles: science, not rule of thumb; harmony, not discord; cooperation, not individualism; maximum output; and developing each worker to the fullest. His techniques: time and motion study, standardisation, functional foremanship and the differential piece-rate wage. |
| Functional foremanship | Taylor split the foreman's job among eight specialists. Four plan the work (route clerk, instruction card clerk, time and cost clerk, shop disciplinarian) and four run the shop floor (gang boss, speed boss, repair boss, inspector). A worker then has several bosses. |
| Fayol's 14 principles | Division of work, authority and responsibility, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction, subordination of individual interest, remuneration, centralisation, scalar chain, order, equity, stability of tenure, initiative and esprit de corps (team spirit). |
| Unity of command and unity of direction | Unity of command: each employee takes orders from one boss only. Unity of direction: one head and one plan for each group of activities with the same aim. Functional foremanship breaks unity of command. |
| Line organisation | Also called military or scalar organisation. Authority runs in a straight line from top to bottom. It is simple and decisions are quick, but there are no specialists and the top is overloaded. |
| Functional organisation | Work is grouped by specialist function, as in Taylor's functional foremanship. Expert supervision, but a worker reports to several bosses. |
| Line and staff organisation | Line managers keep the authority to give orders. Staff specialists (legal, quality, planning) only advise the line; they have no authority over it. Used by most large firms. |
| Matrix organisation | A project organisation laid over a functional one. Each person reports both to a project manager and to a functional head, so there are two bosses. Used for large engineering projects. |
| Delegation and span of control | Delegation means assigning responsibility and authority to a subordinate; the manager stays accountable. Span of control is how many subordinates one manager can supervise well. |
| Motivation theories | Maslow's needs, from the bottom up: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation. Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, working conditions) only prevent dissatisfaction, while motivators (achievement, recognition) motivate. McGregor: Theory X assumes people dislike work; Theory Y assumes they seek it. |
| Wage incentive plans | Halsey plan: bonus of 50% of the time saved. Rowan plan: bonus = time saved ÷ standard time, as a share of time wages. Taylor's differential piece rate: a higher rate per piece for workers who beat the standard. Gantt task and bonus plan. |
| Job evaluation and merit rating | Job evaluation rates the job, not the person, to set fair pay: ranking, grading, points and factor comparison methods. Merit rating rates the person doing the job. |
| Sole proprietorship | One owner who supplies the capital, takes all the profit and has unlimited liability for the debts. |
| Partnership | Two or more partners share capital, profit and management under a partnership deed (Indian Partnership Act, 1932). Liability is unlimited, and joint and several. |
| Joint stock company | A separate legal person owned by shareholders, with limited liability, managed by a board of directors elected by the shareholders. Private limited and public limited companies are both joint stock companies. |
| Cooperative society | Formed by members to serve themselves rather than for profit, with one member, one vote. |
| Public sector undertaking | Owned and controlled by the government: departmental undertakings, public corporations set up by an Act, and government companies. |

### Lists and formulas to memorise

- Fayol's management functions: plan, organise, command, coordinate, control
- Organisation types: line (one chain of command), functional (specialist bosses), line and staff (staff only advise), matrix (two bosses)
- Maslow, bottom to top: physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualisation

```math
\text{Halsey: } E = TR + 0.5\,(S - T)\,R \qquad \text{Rowan: } E = TR + \frac{S - T}{S}\,TR
```

Here E is the earnings, T the time taken, S the standard time and R the hourly rate.

**Q4. Kerala PSC AE (Mechanical), 18 August 2025.** Which of the following is/are part of Taylor's principles of scientific management? ## 10. Forecasting and linear programming

This last sub-topic mixes forecasting with the basic tools of operations research: linear programming, transportation, assignment and queues. Its questions are mostly "which method solves which problem", plus quick sums with exponential smoothing and the M/M/1 queue.

### Main contents and what they mean

| Content | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Forecasting | Estimating future demand, so that production, inventory and staff can be planned. Every plan in sub-topics 3 to 5 starts from a forecast. |
| Qualitative methods | Based on judgement, used when there is little past data, as for a new product. Delphi method: a panel of experts answers in rounds, anonymously, until their views settle on a consensus. Others: market survey, sales force opinion, executive opinion. |
| Time series components | Trend (long-term rise or fall), seasonal (repeats within a year), cyclical (business cycles over several years) and random variation. |
| Moving averages | A simple moving average gives equal weight to the last n periods. A weighted moving average gives each period its own weight, usually more to the recent ones. A longer average is smoother but slower to follow a change. |
| Exponential smoothing | New forecast = α × latest actual + (1 − α) × old forecast, with 0 ≤ α ≤ 1. A high α follows changes quickly; a low α smooths more. It needs only the last forecast and the last actual demand. |
| Regression (least squares) | Fits a straight line y = a + bx to past data (demand against time, or against a cause such as price) and extends it. |
| Forecast error | Mean absolute deviation (MAD) = the average size of the errors (actual − forecast), ignoring their signs. The tracking signal (running sum of errors ÷ MAD) shows whether the forecasts lean one way. |
| Linear programming (LP) | Finds the best value (maximum profit or minimum cost) of a linear objective, subject to linear constraints on resources. Assumptions: linearity (proportionality), additivity, divisibility (variables can take fractional values), certainty of all data and non-negativity. |
| Graphical and simplex methods | With two variables, the constraints are drawn as lines; the feasible region is the area that satisfies all of them, and the best answer lies at one of its corner points. The simplex method (Dantzig) handles any number of variables, adding slack variables for ≤ constraints and surplus and artificial variables for ≥ constraints (Big-M or two-phase method). |
| Duality | Every LP (the primal) has a dual. A maximisation primal gives a minimisation dual; each primal constraint becomes a dual variable, and each primal variable a dual constraint. |
| Transportation problem | Ships goods from m sources to n destinations at least cost. Balanced when total supply = total demand (otherwise add a dummy row or column). A first solution comes from the north-west corner rule, least cost method or Vogel's approximation method (VAM, usually the best start). It is then tested by the MODI (u–v) or stepping-stone method. A non-degenerate solution has m + n − 1 occupied cells. |
| Assignment problem | Assigns n jobs to n machines (or people), one each, at least total cost or time. Solved by the Hungarian method. It is a special case of the transportation problem. |
| Queuing theory | Arrivals at rate λ (Poisson) and service at rate μ (exponential), with one server and first come, first served: the M/M/1 model. The utilisation ρ = λ/μ must be below 1, or the queue grows without limit. |
| Game theory | A two-person zero-sum game: what one player gains, the other loses. If the maximin equals the minimax, the game has a saddle point, and that common value is the value of the game. |

### Formulas to memorise

```math
F_{t+1} = \alpha D_t + (1 - \alpha)F_t = F_t + \alpha\,(D_t - F_t) \qquad SMA_n = \frac{D_t + D_{t-1} + \dots + D_{t-n+1}}{n}
```

```math
\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu} \qquad P_0 = 1 - \rho \qquad L_s = \frac{\lambda}{\mu - \lambda} \qquad L_q = \frac{\lambda^2}{\mu(\mu - \lambda)} \qquad W_s = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda}
```

```math
\text{Occupied cells in a non-degenerate transportation solution} = m + n - 1
```

Here D is the actual demand, F the forecast, P₀ the chance that the server is idle, Lₛ and L\_q the average number in the system and in the queue, and Wₛ the average time spent in the system.

### Past exam questions with solutions

**Q1. Kerala PSC AE (Mechanical) / Inspector of Factories, 22 July 2026.** The forecasting technique of gaining consensus from a group of experts while maintaining their anonymity is: (a) brainstorming (b) expert opinion method (c) Delphi method (d) survey method

*Answer: (c).* In the Delphi method the experts never meet. Each answers a questionnaire, sees a summary of the others' answers and revises, until the views settle. Brainstorming is done face to face, so it is not anonymous.

**Q2. UPPSC AE Mains Mechanical 2024 (Paper I), 29 September 2025.** What will be the forecast for the fourth period from the data below? Period 1: actual demand 105, weightage 0.6. Period 2: 108, weightage 0.3. Period 3: 112, weightage 0.1. (a) 106.6 (b) 108.3 (c) 107.4 (d) 110.1

*Answer: (a).* Weighted moving average = 105 × 0.6 + 108 × 0.3 + 112 × 0.1 = 63 + 32.4 + 11.2 = 106.6. Usually the latest period gets the largest weight, but here the weights are given, so use them as they are. Quick check: the weights add up to 1.

**Q3. TNTRB ME 2017.** In forecasting by exponential smoothing, if α is the smoothing constant, then: (a) new forecast = α (latest sales figure) + (1 − α)(old forecast) (b) new forecast = α (latest sales figure) − (1 − α)(old forecast) (c) new forecast = α (latest sales figure) + (1 + α)(old forecast) (d) new forecast = α (latest sales figure) − (1 + α)(old forecast)

*Answer: (a).* The new forecast is a weighted average of the latest actual and the old forecast, so the two weights, α and 1 − α, must add up to 1. Only option (a) has weights that add up to 1.

**Q4. JKSSB JE ME 2015.** If the actual demand of a product is 62, the previous year's forecast is 57 and the smoothing constant is 0.3, the forecast for the current year by exponential smoothing is: (a) 58.5 (b) 60 (c) 62.5 (d) 65

*Answer: (a).* New forecast = 57 + 0.3 × (62 − 57) = 57 + 1.5 = 58.5. Quick check: the new forecast must lie between the old forecast (57) and the actual demand (62), and with a small α it stays nearer 57.

**Q5. UPPSC AE Mains Mechanical 2024 (Paper I), 29 September 2025.** Which of the following is not an assumption in a general linear programming model? (a) certainty (b) divisibility (c) discreteness (d) linearity

*Answer: (c).* Ordinary LP assumes divisibility: the variables can take fractional values. Discreteness, where variables must be whole numbers, is the opposite assumption, and it belongs to integer programming.

**Q6. Kerala PSC Deputy Engineer / AE (Mechanical), 18 June 2023.** In operations research, the Hungarian method is used to solve: (a) queuing problems (b) assignment problems (c) transportation problems (d) game theory

*Answer: (b).* The Hungarian method solves the one-to-one assignment of jobs to machines. Transportation problems use the north-west corner rule, VAM and MODI. Option (c) is the trap, because assignment is a special case of transportation.

**Q7. TNTRB ME 2012.** Vogel's approximation method is connected with the: (a) assignment problem (b) inventory problem (c) transportation problem (d) PERT

*Answer: (c).* VAM gives a starting solution for a transportation problem, using the penalty (the difference between the two lowest costs) in each row and column. It usually starts closer to the best solution than the north-west corner rule does.

**Q8. Kerala PSC AE (Mechanical), 18 August 2025.** Customers arrive at a hospital at the rate of 5 per hour (Poisson arrivals), and the doctor can serve at the rate of 10 per hour (exponential). What is the probability that a customer does not have to wait and walks straight into the doctor's room? (a) 1.0 (b) 0.5 (c) 0.33 (d) 0.75

*Answer: (b).* A customer walks straight in only when the doctor is free. Utilisation ρ = 5/10 = 0.5, so the doctor is idle with probability P₀ = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5. Option (c) mistakes the formula for λ/(λ + μ) = 5/15.
